Architecture

Monolith or microservices for your startup?

José González, Software architect, October 22, 2026, 7 min read

Short answer

For most startups and MVPs, a modular monolith is the best option: it's faster to build and deploy, cheaper to run, and a small team can understand all of it. Microservices pay off when teams need to deploy independently or when parts of the system have very different needs for scale, security or availability.

The real question isn't technical

When someone tells me "we need microservices", they're almost never describing a problem. They're proposing a solution. Before accepting it, it's worth reframing the question: do we need to deploy parts of the system separately? Is one part getting ten times more load than the rest? Are several teams blocking each other?

If the answer to all of them is "not yet", the architecture should reflect the team and the business you have today, not the one you imagine five years from now.

Quick comparison

CriterionModular monolithMicroservices
Speed to launchHigh: one repository, one deploymentLower: more pieces to coordinate
Running costLow: one database, one pipelineHigh: observability, networking, API versioning
Ideal team sizeFrom 1 to about 10 developersSeveral teams with clear domains
Data consistencyRegular transactionsSagas, events and eventual consistency
Scaling one specific partEverything scales togetherEach service scales on its own

Signs it's time to split out a service

  • One part of the system needs its own release pace, for example payments with specific controls and audits.
  • One part consumes very different resources from the rest, like a matching or image-processing engine.
  • Several teams block each other when deploying, even though the code already has clear boundaries.
  • One part needs availability or security isolation the rest doesn't.

The cost that doesn't show up in the diagram

Ten microservices with two backend developers usually add more coordination than they save. Each service brings a deployment pipeline, logs and traces to correlate, API contracts to version and network failures that didn't exist before. That cost is paid every month, even if the business doesn't need it yet.

The same solution, two structures

To make it concrete, here is the same product (a marketplace with users, missions, payments, matching and notifications) organised both ways in NestJS.

Notice that the business modules are the same in both versions. What changes is everything around them: deployments, networking, contracts and databases. That's why starting with well-separated modules turns a move to microservices into moving folders, not rewriting.

How to start without closing doors

The option I usually recommend is a modular monolith with clear boundaries from day one:

  • Modules by business domain (users, orders, payments), not by technical layer.
  • Hexagonal architecture: the domain doesn't depend on the database or external providers, so changing one piece doesn't force you to rewrite the rest.
  • Modules communicate through interfaces, never by reading another module's tables.
  • Internal events for side effects, which can later move to a real queue.

With that foundation, extracting a module into an independent service becomes a measurable, reversible decision rather than a rewrite.

A real example

In Winners, a missions marketplace I'm building, the first design is a modular NestJS monolith with users, missions, payments, matching and notifications modules, on PostgreSQL with PostGIS. Splitting out payments or matching would only make sense once their control, load or deployment needs genuinely diverge from the rest, and that decision will be documented with its reasons.

Frequently asked questions

Does a monolith scale?

Yes. A well-designed monolith can serve many users by scaling horizontally behind a load balancer. What it can't do is scale a single part on its own.

Is migrating to microservices later expensive?

It depends on how you start. If the monolith has modules with clear boundaries, extracting one is a bounded project. If everything is tangled, it is expensive.

What should I ask a vendor proposing microservices for an MVP?

To explain which concrete problem they solve today, how much they add to the monthly running cost and what would happen if you started with a modular monolith.

Hexagonal architecture and vertical slicing with DDD and CQRS

José González, Software architect, October 26, 2026, 9 min read

Short answer

They are four ideas that fit together: DDD decides which parts the business has and what words describe them; hexagonal architecture protects business rules from technology; vertical slicing organises code by complete features; and CQRS separates what changes data from what only reads it. Together they produce code you can understand, test and change piece by piece.

Four ideas, one sentence each

  • DDD (Domain-Driven Design): the code uses the same language as the business and is split into contexts with clear boundaries, like missions, payments or users.
  • Hexagonal architecture: the domain sits at the centre and talks to the outside through ports; databases, APIs and frameworks are swappable adapters.
  • Vertical slicing: instead of folders by technical layer (all controllers together, all services together), each feature lives in its own folder with everything it needs.
  • CQRS: commands (create, accept, pay) and queries (list, search) follow different paths, each optimised for its job.

1. The hexagon: business at the centre

There is one rule: dependencies point inwards. The domain doesn't know NestJS, PostgreSQL or the payment gateway exist. It defines what it needs through interfaces (ports) and the infrastructure implements them (adapters).

The practical benefit: you can test business rules without a database, and switch payment provider by touching one adapter, not the heart of the system.

2. Vertical slicing: organise by feature

Layers still exist, but you walk through them vertically. Each feature is a slice that cuts through API, application, domain and infrastructure.

When someone on the team needs to change "accept mission", they open one folder and find everything. Two people can work on different features without stepping on each other.

3. CQRS: write and read on different paths

Writing needs rules and consistency; reading needs speed and data shaped for the screen. CQRS separates them: commands go through the aggregate, which enforces the rules, and queries read from a model prepared for them.

You don't need two databases from day one. In many projects it's enough to separate command and query handlers on the same database; a dedicated read model comes only when load or queries justify it.

4. DDD: language and boundaries

DDD is what gives meaning to everything above. Before creating folders, you agree with the business on what things are called and where each context ends. If the business says "accept a mission", the code has an AcceptMission, not an updateStatus. Each context (missions, payments, users) is a module with its own hexagon.

All together in one folder

When it pays off and when it doesn't

  • It pays off when there are real business rules (states, permissions, payments), a team of several people and a product that will live for years.
  • It doesn't for a simple CRUD or a throwaway prototype: there it adds ceremony without benefit.
  • It can be applied in parts: hexagonal in the payments module and something simpler elsewhere is a valid decision, as long as it's documented.

Frequently asked questions

Does CQRS require event sourcing?

No. They are different ideas. CQRS separates reads and writes; event sourcing stores the event history as the source of truth. They can be combined, but most projects don't need it.

Are hexagonal and clean architecture the same?

They share the same rule: dependencies point towards the domain. The names and number of rings change, not the idea.

Does this fit a monolith?

Yes, and that's where it works best at first: a modular monolith built on these principles can later be split into services without rewriting the domain.

AI and quality

AI-assisted design and development: the architect as auditor

José González, Software architect, October 26, 2026, 7 min read

Short answer

AI assistants write code very fast, but they don't know your business, your past decisions or the cost of maintaining what they propose. To stop that speed from turning into technical debt, someone has to set the context beforehand and audit the result afterwards. That role fits the software architect naturally.

What AI does well and what it doesn't

An assistant is excellent at producing a first version, explaining unfamiliar code, writing tests, refactoring and documenting. It falls short on what isn't in the text it receives: why the team chose a pattern, what legal constraints the product has, which module must not depend on which, or what will happen to that code two years from now.

The real risks

  • Plausible but wrong code: it compiles, looks reasonable and fails on the edge case the business cares about.
  • Architecture that dissolves: each proposal solves its task its own way and, after a hundred changes, the system no longer follows any pattern.
  • Security and dependencies: outdated libraries, missing validation or secrets where they shouldn't be.
  • Teams that stop understanding their code: if nobody reviews with judgement, knowledge stays in the AI conversation instead of the team.

The architect as auditor

The goal isn't to slow down AI use but to give it a frame. The architect steps in twice: before, by providing context, and after, by reviewing against clear criteria.

Before: provide context

  • Written architecture decisions (ADRs) that both the team and the assistant can consult.
  • Repository guidelines: folder structure, conventions, approved libraries.
  • Reference examples: "this is how a use case is built here".

After: audit with a clear checklist

  • Does it respect module boundaries and the direction of dependencies?
  • Does it meet the business rule, including edge cases?
  • Does it have tests that would fail if the rule broke?
  • Is it secure: input validation, permissions, secrets, dependencies?
  • Can it be observed in production: logs and errors with context?
  • Would someone else on the team understand it six months from now?

What the team gains

  • Consistent quality: the same rules for hand-written and AI-proposed code.
  • Maintainability: the system keeps a recognisable architecture even with many hands and many assistants.
  • Teamwork: reviews turn AI proposals into shared knowledge.
  • Control: every important decision has a human owner and is documented.

How I apply it

I use AI assistants daily to analyse requirements, design, refactor, generate tests and document. The rule is always the same: the AI proposes and a person decides. Decisions go into ADRs, code goes through review and the team's standards, and what isn't understood doesn't get merged.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't this slow development down?

It adds a review, but it avoids the much bigger cost of fixing code nobody understands later. With good context upfront, the review is usually quick.

Do I need an architect if my team is small?

You need the role, not necessarily a job title. In a small team, the person with the strongest technical judgement can take it on if they have time set aside for it.

How to integrate two SaaS platforms so it doesn't break when one fails

José González, Software architect, October 26, 2026, 8 min read

Short answer

Assume the other platform will fail. Isolate each API behind a connector, orchestrate the process with a workflow that keeps state, retry with growing waits, make every operation idempotent so it can be repeated without duplicates, and log every step with context. That way a failure becomes a delay, not lost data.

The problem: two platforms that don't talk

At Emergya I was the architect for integrating two platforms used by Publicis: Encodify, for marketing content management, and Workfront, for project management. The goal was for information to flow between them automatically and reliably, without anyone copying data by hand.

The challenge isn't calling an API. It's what happens when that API responds late, returns a temporary error or goes down halfway through a multi-step process. If the integration isn't ready for it, you get half-written records, duplicates or, worse, data that disappears without anyone noticing.

The architecture

  • Isolated connectors: each platform has its own NestJS connector with hexagonal architecture. The integration domain doesn't know each API's details; if one changes, only its adapter is touched.
  • A workflow that orchestrates: multi-step processes are orchestrated with Google Cloud Workflows, which keeps the state of each run and retries the steps that fail.
  • Clear contracts with other teams: the Spring Boot services and the NestJS connectors were aligned through API contracts agreed before coding.

1. Retries with exponential backoff

Most failures in third-party APIs are temporary: overload, a deployment, a network blip. Retrying instantly only makes the overload worse. The rule is to wait longer each time and add a little randomness so thousands of retries don't arrive at once.

Knowing when not to retry matters just as much: a 400 error caused by invalid data won't fix itself by waiting. Those cases are logged and set aside for review.

2. Idempotency: repeat without duplicating

If you retry, sooner or later you'll process the same message twice: the operation worked, but the response got lost on the way back. Idempotency guarantees that repeating an operation produces the same result as doing it once.

In practice: each message carries a unique key (for example, the resource ID and its version), and before processing it you check whether it was already processed. When the target API allows it, use "create or update" instead of "create".

3. Stateful workflows

An integration process usually has several steps: read the resource, transform it, create it in the target, link it and notify. If step four fails, you don't want to repeat the first three or leave the process half-done. A workflow records which step each run is on, retries only what failed and leaves a record of how it ended.

4. Observability: know what happened

A silent integration is a dangerous one. Every step writes logs with the run ID and the resource ID, so a specific case can be followed end to end. In the project we used Cloud Logging to detect and diagnose errors in production. Failures that exhaust their retries must stay visible somewhere someone reviews and can reprocess them.

Checklist

  • Each external API sits behind its own connector.
  • Multi-step processes keep their state.
  • Retries wait longer each time and tell temporary errors from data errors.
  • Every operation is idempotent and has a unique key.
  • Logs let you follow a specific case from start to finish.
  • Permanent failures stay visible and can be reprocessed.
  • API contracts are agreed and versioned with the other teams.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a message queue for this?

Not always. A stateful workflow with retries covers many cases. A queue pays off when volume is high or when you want to fully decouple the pace of the two platforms.

How many times should I retry?

It depends on the business: how long the data can wait and how long the API usually takes to recover. What matters is having a limit and a clear place where cases that exceed it end up.

What if the platform doesn't support idempotent operations?

Keep a record on your side of which keys were processed successfully and check it before each operation. That's the processed table in the diagram.

When do AI agents make sense for your company?

José González, Software architect, October 26, 2026, 7 min read

Short answer

An AI agent makes sense when the task is different every time (fixed rules can't solve it), there's enough context for the AI to decide well, and the cost of a mistake is acceptable or controlled by a person approving the key steps. If the task is repetitive, classic automation is cheaper and more reliable.

What an agent is, without the hype

An assistant answers when you ask. An agent receives a goal and decides the steps: it looks up data, uses tools, chains actions and checks its own result. That autonomy is what makes it useful, and also what makes it risky.

A matrix to decide

Two questions are enough to place almost any case: does the task change every time or is it always the same? How much does it cost if it goes wrong?

  • Repetitive and cheap errors: no AI needed. A script, a rule or a workflow does it better and cheaper.
  • Variable and cheap errors: good ground for an autonomous agent, for example classifying and summarising internal documents, with spot checks.
  • Repetitive and costly errors: deterministic rules decide; AI only prepares data or drafts that a person validates.
  • Variable and costly errors: a supervised agent. The AI proposes and a person approves each important step.

Where they genuinely help

  • Turning messy information (emails, documents, notes) into structured data.
  • Preparing first versions: proposals, analyses, data models, code.
  • Handling requests within a defined flow, with a handover to a person when needed.

Where they don't belong

  • Processes already solved with clear rules.
  • Decisions with legal or financial impact without an accountable person.
  • Cases without data or context: the agent will decide blindly.

How to keep a person in charge

  • Explicit approval points on steps that commit money, data or customers.
  • Least privilege: the agent only reaches the tools it needs.
  • A record of every decision and the data behind it.
  • A simple way to stop, correct and relaunch.
  • Quality metrics reviewed by people, not just by the agent itself.

An example of my own

In App Factory, a project of my own, I'm designing a chain of agents that takes a business idea to rules, a data model, code and a launch plan. Each stage ends at a human review point before moving on: the agents speed up the work, but the decisions stay with a person.

Frequently asked questions

Is a chatbot an agent?

Not necessarily. A chatbot that answers questions is an assistant. It becomes an agent when it can take actions and chain steps on its own.

Where do I start?

With a specific, bounded and measurable process in the cheap-error quadrant. With real results, you decide whether it's worth expanding.

What a software architect does and when to hire one

José González, Software architect, October 26, 2026, 6 min read

Short answer

A software architect translates business goals into technical decisions the team can build and operate: which parts the system has, how they communicate, which risks are accepted and why. It's worth hiring one before important decisions start being made by themselves, out of inertia or urgency.

A bridge, not a tower

An architect doesn't design alone and hand over a diagram. They sit between the business, which knows what it needs, and the team, which knows how to build it, and make sure what reaches production answers the first and can be maintained by the second.

What they do day to day

  • Understand the business: processes, users, functional and non-functional requirements, risks and constraints.
  • Decide and document: architecture, alternatives and cost, with every decision explained in an ADR.
  • Coordinate: work allocation, standards, code review and contract alignment between teams.
  • Watch production: observability, resilience and security, adjusting the architecture with what they learn.

Signs your project needs one

  • Each new feature takes longer than the last.
  • Production errors keep coming back and nobody quite knows why.
  • You need to integrate several systems or external providers.
  • Several teams or vendors work on the same product.
  • Nobody can explain why the system is built the way it is.
  • You're investing in a product that has to live for years, not months.

When not yet

In a prototype to validate an idea, or a small product with a single senior developer, that person can take on the role. What matters is that someone has time set aside to think about the architecture, not the job title.

Architect, tech lead or engineering manager

RoleMain focusQuestion it answers
Software architectSystem structure and technical decisionsHow do we build it so it lasts and can change?
Tech leadTeam delivery and code qualityHow do we build it well this iteration?
Engineering managerPeople, processes and growthDoes the team have what it needs to work well?

In small teams these roles overlap, and it's common for one person to combine architecture and technical leadership.

Frequently asked questions

Can it be part-time?

Yes. For many companies an architect who sets direction, reviews key decisions and supports the team a few hours a week makes sense.

What should they deliver in the first weeks?

A map of the current system, the main risks prioritised and the first documented decisions, with a plan to address them.

AI voice assistants: from prototype to production

José González, Software architect, October 26, 2026, 7 min read

Short answer

A voice assistant is a chain: speech-to-text, intent understanding, business logic and text-to-speech. A prototype validates that the conversation works; production also demands short response times, handover to a person, security, testing with real conversations and monitoring.

The context

At Emergya I built the working prototype of the phone version of Blue, BBVA's virtual assistant, to validate the voice channel, and developed new assistant features in a regulated banking environment. This article sums up what matters along that path, without going into the client's internal details.

How it works inside

  • Speech-to-text: turns what the user says into text. Noise, accents and cut-off sentences are the norm, not the exception.
  • Dialogflow CX: identifies the intent and manages the conversation flow with pages and states, including what to do when it doesn't understand.
  • Node.js backend: receives Dialogflow's requests (webhooks), looks up data and applies the business rules.
  • Text-to-speech: returns the answer in a natural voice.

What a prototype validates

  • That users understand what they can ask for over the phone.
  • That the main flows complete from start to finish.
  • That the technical chain works with real speech, not just typed text.

What production demands

  • Low latency across the whole chain: on a call, long silences are far more noticeable than in a chat.
  • Handover to a person when the assistant doesn't understand or the case requires it.
  • Answers written for the ear: short sentences and confirmations before important actions.
  • Security and privacy suited to a regulated environment.
  • Testing with real conversations, including recognition errors.
  • Monitoring of unrecognised intents and drop-offs to improve the flows.

Design for voice, don't adapt the chat

A flow that works in text doesn't work the same on the phone. Voice has no buttons or on-screen lists, the user can't reread and interrupts mid-sentence. That's why voice flows are designed separately, even when they share backend and rules with the text channel.

Frequently asked questions

Can the chat assistant be reused for voice?

The backend, rules and many intents can be reused. The wording and conversation flows are best redesigned for voice.

Dialogflow CX or a generative language model?

It depends on the risk. In banking, controlled flows are the foundation; generative AI can help in bounded parts, always with clear limits.

Transport, Ecuador, Devesofft, 2018–2019

Rubí: bus tickets from the counter to the phone, built for the busiest day of the year.

Tech lead and architect, B2C and B2B platform for ticket sales, bookings, parcel shipping and billing

My roleTech lead and solution architect
Team6 people: 3 developers, design, sales and me
CustomersTravellers (B2C) and transport companies (B2B)
DurationAbout two years, from market study to production

The business problem

Ecuador's bus terminals host many transport companies at once, running interprovincial routes around the clock. On peak days (holidays, Fridays and long weekends) counter sales become a bottleneck: long queues and little visibility of which seats are still free on each bus.

The goal was twofold: let travellers buy a ticket and pick a seat without depending on the counter, and let each transport company manage routes, sales, parcels and billing from a single tool.

First, understand the business

  • Market study of how most of the country's transport companies operate.
  • Field research at terminals and interviews with people in the industry.
  • Brainstorming and analysis sessions to decide what went into the product and what didn't.
  • User experience designed around the transport companies' daily work, for both passengers and parcels.

What we built

  • Online ticket sales with integrated payment gateways.
  • Real-time seat selection over socket connections: every user sees the same bus map instantly.
  • Route and stop design on Google Maps and live tracking of journeys.
  • Management modules for transport companies: passengers, parcels and daily operations.
  • Integration with external billing systems.

Architecture decisions

ADR · Why a monolith with horizontal and vertical scaling

Context. A small team, private funding from the owner himself and an MVP that already had a lot of functionality. The most expensive risk wasn't technical: it was going live and failing on a peak-demand day.

Decision. A Laravel monolith deployed on AWS Linux machines behind load balancers, scaling by adding machines (horizontal) or making them bigger (vertical).

Consequences. One deployment, one codebase and one place to monitor, manageable for three developers. The real complexity, concurrency, is solved in the domain design rather than by splitting the system into services.

  • Concurrency under control: we analysed race conditions and idempotency so two people can never end up with the same seat and a retry or double click never causes a double charge.
  • Continuous deployment on AWS, with monitoring and logs to detect and diagnose problems in production.
  • Security: load balancers and AWS security services, plus protection against XSS, CSRF and SQL injection.
